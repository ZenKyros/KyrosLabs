## Vector Space

A **vector space** is a collection of vectors where:

- vectors can be added
- vectors can be multiplied by scalars
- the results remain within the space

Example:

$$\mathbb{R}^2 = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} : x, y \in \mathbb{R} \right\}$$


If

$$\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$
then:

$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\text{and:}$$


$$5\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}$$

Both are still vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
### Important idea

A vector space is the **environment in which vectors exist and vector operations are valid**.

Common vector spaces:

$$\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \mathbb{R}^3, \qquad \mathbb{R}^n$$


## Subspace

A **subspace** is a subset of a vector space that is itself a vector space.

If $W \subseteq V$, then $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if:

### Zero vector 
$$\mathbf{0} \in W$$

### Closed under addition
$$\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W \implies \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W$$

### Closed under scalar multiplication
$$\mathbf{u} \in W, \quad c \in \mathbb{R} \implies c\mathbf{u} \in W$$

These three conditions are the **subspace test**.



## Linear Combination

A linear combination of vectors is:

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n$$

where $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n$ are scalars.

Example:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then:

$$3\mathbf{v}_1 + 5\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

The numbers $3$ and $5$ are the **coefficients**.

---


## Span

The **span** of a set of vectors is the set of all possible linear combinations of those vectors.

$$\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n) = \left\{ c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n : c_i \in \mathbb{R} \right\}$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then:

$$\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = \mathbb{R}^2$$

because every vector

$$\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$$

can be written as:

$$x\mathbf{v}_1 + y\mathbf{v}_2$$

### Core meaning

$$\boxed{\text{Span = everything that can be generated by the vectors}}$$

## Linear Independence

Vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n$ are **linearly independent** if the equation:

$$c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n = \mathbf{0}$$

has only the trivial solution:

$$c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_n = 0$$

If a non-zero set of coefficients can produce the zero vector, the vectors are **linearly dependent**.

### Example

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since:

$$\mathbf{v}_2 = 2\mathbf{v}_1$$

the vectors are **linearly dependent**. 

One vector does not provide a new independent direction.


## Basis

A **basis** is a set of vectors that is:

1. **Linearly independent**
2. **Spans the space**

Therefore:

$$\boxed{ \text{Basis} = \text{Independent vectors that span the space} }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Every vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ can be uniquely written as:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x\mathbf{e}_1 + y\mathbf{e}_2$$

 **Key idea**

A basis is a set of **independent building blocks** for a space.



## Dimension

The **dimension** of a vector space is the number of vectors in any basis of that space.

Examples:

$$\dim(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2, \qquad \dim(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3, \qquad \dim(\mathbb{R}^n) = n$$

If:

$$W = \operatorname{span} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

then:

$$\dim(W) = 2$$


## Basis and Dimension

These two ideas are directly connected:

$$\boxed{ \text{Basis} \rightarrow \text{independent directions} } \qquad \boxed{ \text{Dimension} \rightarrow \text{number of independent directions} }$$

For example, if a space has a basis:

$$\{\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\}$$

then:

$$\dim(V) = 3$$


## Norm

A **norm** measures the size or magnitude of a vector.

Notation:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|$$

For:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}$$

there are several important norms.


### 1 Norm

The L1 norm is:

$$\boxed{ \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = |x_1| + |x_2| + \cdots + |x_n| }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = |3| + |-4| + |2| = 3 + 4 + 2 = 9$$

---

### L2 Norm

The L2 norm is the standard Euclidean length.

$$\boxed{ \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2} }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$$

---

###  L∞ Norm

The L∞ norm is the largest absolute component:

$$\boxed{ \|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = \max_i |x_i| }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -8 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Therefore:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty = 8$$



Remember:

$$\boxed{ L_1 = \text{sum of absolute values} } \qquad \boxed{ L_2 = \text{Euclidean length} } \qquad \boxed{ L_\infty = \text{largest absolute value} }$$



## Distance

Distance measures how far apart two vectors are.

For vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$, Euclidean distance is:

$$\boxed{ d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2 }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

First:

$$\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then:

$$d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + (-4)^2} = 5$$

---

## Manhattan Distance

Manhattan distance uses the L1 norm:

$$\boxed{ d_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_1 }$$

For the same vectors:

$$\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Therefore:

$$d_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = |-3| + |-4| = 3 + 4 = 7$$

---


## Dot Product

For:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix}$$

the dot product is:

$$\boxed{ \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_iy_i }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then:

$$\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y} = 1(4) + 2(5) + 3(6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32$$




## 17. Angle Between Vectors

The dot product is related to the angle between two vectors:

$$\boxed{ \mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y} = \|\mathbf{x}\|_2\|\mathbf{y}\|_2\cos\theta }$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2\|\mathbf{y}\|_2} }$$

This gives **cosine similarity**.

where:
* $\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}$ is the **dot product** (inner product) of the two vectors.
* $\|\mathbf{x}\|_2$ and $\|\mathbf{y}\|_2$ are the **$L_2$ norms** (Euclidean lengths) of vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$.
* $\theta$ is the **angle** between the two vectors.


## Cosine Similarity

Cosine similarity measures how similar the **directions** of two vectors are.

$$\boxed{ \operatorname{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2\|\mathbf{y}\|_2} }$$

Range:

$$-1 \leq \operatorname{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \leq 1$$

### Interpretation

| Value | Meaning |
| :---: | --- |
| $1$ | Same direction |
| $0$ | Perpendicular (Orthogonal) |
| $-1$ | Opposite direction |



# 19. Example: Cosine Similarity

Let:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Dot product:

$$\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y} = 1(2) + 1(2) = 4$$

Norms:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}, \qquad \|\mathbf{y}\|_2 = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$$

Therefore:

$$\text{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2} \cdot 2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{4}{4} = 1$$

They point in exactly the same direction.

---


## Magnitude vs Direction

Consider:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

and:

$$\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 100 \\ 100 \end{bmatrix}$$

Their magnitudes are very different.

But:

$$\text{cos\_sim}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = 1$$

because their directions are identical.

Therefore:

$$\boxed{ \text{Norm} \rightarrow \text{magnitude} } \qquad \boxed{ \text{Euclidean distance} \rightarrow \text{separation} } \qquad \boxed{ \text{Cosine similarity} \rightarrow \text{directional similarity} }$$

## Unit Vector / Normalization

A unit vector has a length of $1$.

To normalize $\mathbf{x}$:

$$\boxed{ \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_2} }$$

Example:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = 5$$

the normalized vector is:

$$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 3/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$$

and:

$$\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|_2 = 1$$

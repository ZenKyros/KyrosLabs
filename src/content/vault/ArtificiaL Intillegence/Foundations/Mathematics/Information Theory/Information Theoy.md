## Information

Information theory studies **how information is measured and represented under uncertainty**.

For an event with probability $P(x)$, its self-information is:

$$\boxed{ I(x) = -\log P(x) }$$

where:
* $I(x)$ is the **self-information** (or surprisal) of the specific outcome $x$.
* $P(x)$ is the probability of outcome $x$ occurring ($0 \le P(x) \le 1$).
* $\log$ represents the logarithm operator. When base 2 ($\log_2$) is chosen, information is measured in **bits**. When the natural log ($\ln$) is chosen, it is measured in **nats**.

### Intuition
Rare events contain significantly more information than common events.
* **High probability** $\rightarrow$ low information (low surprise).
* **Low probability** $\rightarrow$ high information (high surprise).

## Entropy

Entropy measures the average **uncertainty** or expected information contained across an entire probability distribution.

$$\boxed{ H(X) = -\sum_x P(x) \log P(x) }$$

where:
* $H(X)$ is the **entropy** of the discrete random variable $X$.
* $\sum_x$ represents the summation operator over all possible distinct states or outcomes $x$.
* $P(x)$ is the probability mass function evaluating the likelihood of outcome $x$.

### Intuition
> Higher entropy = more uncertainty.

Example:
A completely fair coin with $P(\text{Heads}) = 0.5, P(\text{Tails}) = 0.5$ has maximum uncertainty (highest entropy), whereas a heavily biased coin that yields heads 99% of the time has very low uncertainty (low entropy).

### AI Connection
Entropy serves as a foundational building block for:
* Classification objectives
* Information Gain selectors in Decision Trees
* Evaluating token distributions in Language Models
* Optimization targets in Probabilistic Machine Learning



## Cross-Entropy

Cross-entropy measures the average number of bits needed to identify an event from a true probability distribution if we use an estimated or predicted distribution instead.

$$\boxed{ H(P, Q) = -\sum_x P(x) \log Q(x) }$$

where:
* $H(P, Q)$ is the **cross-entropy** calculated between the target distribution and the model's estimate.
* $P$ is the **true underlying probability distribution** (often represented as ground-truth labels).
* $Q$ is the **predicted probability distribution** generated by the model.
* $P(x)$ is the true probability of outcome $x$.
* $Q(x)$ is the model's assigned probability for outcome $x$.

### Classification Loss Shorthand
For a categorical machine learning classification framework utilizing one-hot encoded targets, the loss calculation over a single sample simplifies to:

$$\boxed{ L = -\sum_i y_i \log(\hat{y}_i) }$$

where:
* $L$ is the **Cross-Entropy Loss** score.
* $\sum_i$ accumulates values over all possible class indices $i$.
* $y_i$ is the actual target label for class $i$ ($1$ for the correct class, $0$ for all incorrect classes).
* $\hat{y}_i$ is the model's predicted probability for class $i$ (typically generated via a Softmax layer).

### Key Intuition
* If the model assigns a high probability to the correct class $\rightarrow$ **low loss**.
* If the model assigns a low probability to the correct class $\rightarrow$ **high loss**.

**Cross-entropy loss is one of the most critical optimization loss metrics in modern AI engineering.**



## KL Divergence

Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence measures the directional divergence or relative entropy between one probability distribution and a baseline distribution.

$$\boxed{ D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_x P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} }$$

where:
* $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q)$ is the **KL Divergence** of distribution $Q$ from distribution $P$.
* $\parallel$ is the structural math delimiter representing the comparison channel between the two systems.
* $P(x)$ is the baseline probability of outcome $x$ from the reference distribution $P$.
* $Q(x)$ is the corresponding probability of outcome $x$ from the approximating distribution $Q$.
* $\dfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ is the ratio of their respective probabilities at point $x$.

### Properties
KL Divergence is strictly non-negative:

$$\boxed{ D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \ge 0 }$$

However, because the directional evaluation changes when swapping the order of the inputs:

$$\boxed{ D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \neq D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P) }$$

Therefore, KL divergence is **not symmetric**, meaning it cannot be treated as a true geometric distance metric.

### Intuition
> *"How much unique information is lost when we use distribution $Q$ to approximate distribution $P$?"*

### AI Uses
* Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) to penalize the latent space bottleneck
* Knowledge Distillation to match teacher-student logit distributions
* Contrastive distribution alignment tasks


## Jensen-Shannon Divergence

Jensen-Shannon (JS) Divergence is an advanced, symmetric, and bounded modification of KL divergence that addresses asymmetry and infinite penalty issues.

First, define the intermediate average midpoint distribution matrix $M$:

$$\boxed{ M = \frac{P + Q}{2} }$$

where:
* $M$ is the structural **mixture distribution** vector.
* $P$ and $Q$ are the two original input probability distributions being cross-evaluated.

Then, the JS Divergence calculation is defined as:

$$\boxed{ \text{JS}(P, Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M) }$$

where:
* $\text{JS}(P, Q)$ is the final **Jensen-Shannon Divergence** score.
* $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M)$ is the directional KL divergence from $P$ to the midpoint mixture $M$.
* $D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M)$ is the directional KL divergence from $Q$ to the midpoint mixture $M$.

### Properties
* It is completely **symmetric**, satisfying:

$$\boxed{ \text{JS}(P, Q) = \text{JS}(Q, P) }$$

* It is strictly non-negative and is bounded between $0$ and $1$ when using base-2 logs ($0 \le \text{JS}(P, Q) \le 1$).
* It remains mathematically stable even if the distributions do not share overlapping support regions.

### AI Connection
Historically important in **Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)**, where minimizing the original minimax loss objective is mathematically equivalent to minimizing the JS Divergence between the synthetic distribution and the real training data distribution.


## Mutual Information

Mutual Information quantifies the absolute amount of information shared between two random variables by evaluating how much information is obtained about one variable by observing the other.

$$\boxed{ I(X; Y) = \sum_{x,y} P(x,y) \log \frac{P(x,y)}{P(x)P(y)} }$$

where:
* $I(X; Y)$ is the **mutual information** score between random variables $X$ and $Y$.
* $\sum_{x,y}$ is a joint summation over all possible combinations of outcomes $x$ and $y$.
* $P(x,y)$ is the **joint probability distribution** of $X$ and $Y$ occurring together.
* $P(x)$ and $P(y)$ are the individual, marginal probability distributions of each variable.

### Alternative Structural Identity
Mutual Information can be elegantly derived via individual and conditional entropies:

$$\boxed{ I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) }$$

where:
* $H(X)$ is the baseline baseline entropy of variable $X$.
* $H(X \mid Y)$ is the **conditional entropy** of $X$ given that the value of $Y$ has been completely observed.

### Intuition
> *"How much does knowing the exact value of $Y$ reduce our overall uncertainty regarding the value of $X$?"*

If variables $X$ and $Y$ are completely independent, knowing one yields zero new information about the other, making their joint distribution equal to the product of their marginals ($P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)$), which simplifies the calculation to zero:

$$\boxed{ I(X; Y) = 0 }$$

### AI Uses
* Non-linear feature selection pipelines
* Representation learning frameworks
* Information Bottleneck optimization methods to trim redundant feature layers


## Perplexity

Perplexity measures how effectively a probability model or language generator predicts a specific sequence of evaluation data.

$$\boxed{ \text{PP} = e^{-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(x_i)} }$$

where:
* $\text{PP}$ is the final calculated **Perplexity** score.
* $N$ is the total count of tokens or items in the evaluation sequence.
* $\sum_{i=1}^{N}$ aggregates values sequentially over each evaluated token position $i$.
* $P(x_i)$ is the probability assigned by the model to the true token $x_i$, conditioned on all prior historical context tokens.
* $e$ is Euler's number, representing natural exponential base formatting.

### Alternative Base-2 System
When a framework utilizes base-2 logarithms to evaluate its token probabilities, the equivalent formula swaps to a base-2 power scale:

$$\boxed{ \text{PP} = 2^{-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log_2 P(x_i)} }$$

where:
* $\log_2$ represents the base-2 logarithm operator.

### Simple Cross-Entropy Form
Perplexity can also be succinctly expressed as an exponentiation of the model's average cross-entropy score:

$$\boxed{ \text{PP} = e^H }$$

where:
* $H$ is the average cross-entropy score calculated per token using natural log units.

### Language Models
Perplexity measures how "surprised" or confused a language model is when it reads test text sequences. 
* **Lower perplexity scores indicate significantly better predictive performance on a comparable validation dataset.**

Example:
* **Model A:** Perplexity = $20$
* **Model B:** Perplexity = $50$

Model A has lower perplexity, meaning it assigns higher overall probability to the true test text sequence and is much more confident in its token generations under identical evaluation conditions.
